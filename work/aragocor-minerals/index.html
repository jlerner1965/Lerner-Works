Client project

AragoCor Minerals

A marketing site for an industrial mineral supplier selling one material, in four defined grades, to buyers in very different industries, and the AI sourcing tool built to turn visitors into quote requests.

In short

  • The problem. One mineral sold to very different buyers, from soil agronomists to glass engineers, who share almost no vocabulary and check every number.
  • What I built. A site organized by grade and by buyer, with the sizing and documents each one checks first and "Request bulk pricing" on every page, plus a freight and quote tool on its own URL.
  • What it means for you. If you serve several kinds of customer, each gets a page in their own terms, all pointing to the one action that pays.

The situation

AragoCor supplies Bahamian oolitic aragonite into very different markets: agriculture, glass, water treatment, polymers and coatings, and a longer tail that runs from aquaculture to construction. Same mineral, buyers who care about entirely different things. A soil agronomist and a glass batch engineer share almost no vocabulary.

Most industrial supplier sites solve this by going vague — a stock photo, "quality solutions for your needs," and a contact form. That loses the technical buyer, who is scanning for one thing: does this material meet my spec?

The site needed to be specific enough to satisfy an engineer and organized enough that five audiences could each find their own room.

Constraints

The buyer is technical and skeptical, so vague claims actively lose the sale. Eleven application areas had to be served from one product line without the site fragmenting into eleven sites, and every figure had to survive a buyer checking it against a lab report. And anything added later — including the sourcing tool — had to drop onto the finished site without a rebuild.

What I built

A structure organized around the buyer, not the org chart. Products are graded by process — AG-CAL, GL-CAL, WT-CAL, PL-CAL — each carrying the published sizing a buyer actually checks for: 0.2–0.5 mm, under 0.1 mm, 1–3 mm, and for the filler grade, classification to the buyer's own written specification. Each grade has its own controlled data sheet and its own quote and sample request. Industries gets its own path, eleven pages, for buyers who don't yet know which grade they need, and it says which of them have a grade stocked for them and which are served by custom classification. Science holds the technical depth; Resources holds the data sheets, marked as the place procurement starts.

Copy with numbers in it, and only numbers that can be traced. 96.17% CaCO₃, stated as what it is: the representative analysis of one identified sample, by ASTM C 25, from a named lab report, and not a guaranteed minimum. Sizing given grade by grade, each labeled a published range. No kiln, no calcination step. OMRI listed, with both listing numbers and what each one does and does not cover. Buying written out in four plain steps, from choosing a grade or sending a specification to a delivered quote, and the supply chain named company by company rather than gestured at. Writing this required understanding the material, not just restyling a brief.

A visual system that stays credible. Open-ocean imagery, editorial serif headlines, generous whitespace. Restrained enough to read as a serious industrial supplier rather than a startup.

Conversion built into the frame. "Request bulk pricing" sits in the header on every page — the single action the site exists to drive — and every other ask on the site, a quote or a sample from a grade card, lands on the same form with the request and the grade already filled in. Each request is also passed straight into AragoCor's own system, so the first reply starts from what the buyer asked.

Then: the sourcing tool

The site answered what AragoCor sells. Buyers still stalled on the next question — what it costs to move a container from origin to their port — and every one of those went to a salesperson, who spent the day re-answering the basics.

So I built a second layer: a searchable mineral catalog, a maritime freight and stowage calculator that returns real shipping figures, and RFQ capture that routes to sales with the conversation already attached, so the first human reply can be an actual quote. It runs on live AI with an offline knowledge engine behind it, so it degrades instead of failing when a provider is unreachable. It was built to drop onto the existing site as a floating widget, with no rebuild.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Specificity over polish. A measured CaCO₃ figure and four grade codes in the hero, not "premium quality minerals." That narrows the audience and converts the part of it that matters.

Withdraw a claim rather than defend it. Earlier copy said 96–98% CaCO₃ and gave one 20-to-325-mesh range across every grade. The range of purity had come off a third party's letterhead rather than a test of this material, and the mesh range was not true of the coarsest grade, which is coarser than a No. 20 sieve. Both were replaced with what can be traced — one lab report's figure, sizing grade by grade — and both now sit on a register of withdrawn claims that the site's own validator fails on, so neither can creep back in. When the nine one-page application brochures turned out to still carry claims the site had dropped, they came down on October 2 and their URLs were redirected to the matching pages.

A second answer path, at a cost. Building the offline fallback took real time up front. It also means a sales tool that stays up when a third-party API doesn't — a fair trade for something sitting on the revenue path.

Standalone, not embedded. The tool went live on its own URL first, so the freight logic could be tested against real use before it appeared in front of existing customers. In September the decision was made not to have a chat assistant on the main site at all, and the one that had been built into it was removed, so standalone is now the answer rather than a stage.

Calculator over chatbot. The AI is the interface; the freight number is the product. Plenty of tools will chat with a buyer. Far fewer give them a number they can act on.

Where it stands

The site is live at aragocorminerals.com, with conversion events on every step of the quote path — request started, submitted, completed — so the question of what's working is answered with data rather than opinion. In September the analytics moved from Plausible to Google Analytics alongside Vercel's own; Google's side has recorded since October 8, when a content-security rule that had been blocking it was fixed.

Since the last check, the site has gained a blog, nine posts written for industrial buyers, from October 8, and lost the nine application brochures, for the reason above. The sourcing tool runs on its own URL, and as of October 11, 2026 the main site carries no widget and no chat assistant.

What this means for your project

Most local businesses are not selling one mineral into five industries. The problem underneath is common, though: one business, several kinds of customer, and a site that has to be specific to each of them without becoming five sites.

  • One page per kind of customer, each written in that customer's own terms, so a visitor lands somewhere that already sounds like their problem.
  • The numbers in the copy, and where each one comes from. Prices, sizes, lead times, the towns you serve. The specifics that make a skeptical reader trust a page are the ones most sites leave out, and the ones that cost the most when they turn out to be wrong.
  • One action, everywhere. Whatever your equivalent of "Request bulk pricing" is, it sits in the header of every page and nothing else competes with it.
